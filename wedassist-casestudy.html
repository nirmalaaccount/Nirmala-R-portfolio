01 / Product context

Wedding planning involves more than finding a vendor.

Couples navigate many service categories, coordinate with different vendors and make high-stakes decisions about cost, timing and trust. WedAssist combines an online selection and payment experience with help from a dedicated Relationship Manager.

01 / CHOICE

Multiple services

Couples need a way to understand available assistance and choose the categories relevant to their wedding.

02 / COORDINATION

Many moving parts

Vendor communication, recommendations and booking support need to connect to the choices made online.

03 / CONFIDENCE

High-stakes decisions

Before paying, users need to understand their selection, the amount due and what happens next.

Design challenge — How might we make assisted wedding planning feel as straightforward as booking a service online, without losing the reassurance of human support?

02 / My role & constraints

Turning product requirements into a complete experience.

The product team provided requirements and direction. My responsibility was to translate them into usable screens, connected interactions and the states needed to make the journey work beyond the happy path.

What I owned

UX/UI design for service and package selection, category drawers, contact verification, wedding details, checkout, payment feedback and handoff-related messaging.

How the team contributed

One connected journey, from choice to human assistance.

Instead of asking users to complete one long form, the experience progresses through smaller decisions, with details requested when they become relevant.

04 / Key design decisions

Four decisions that shaped the experience.

These are the areas where the product requirements became specific interface patterns, interaction rules and feedback states.

Decision 01 · Service selection

Make the offer understandable before asking users to commit.

Lead with assisted-service packages, while preserving an alternative path for users who want individual services.

Design rationale

Separating packages from individual services helps users understand two distinct ways of buying assistance before moving into detailed selection.

Decision 02 · Focused customization

Keep service-category selection in context.

A side drawer presents the available categories and gives immediate visual feedback as selections are made.

Design rationale

The drawer keeps users anchored to their chosen package. A disabled-to-enabled submit state communicates when the selection requirement is met.

Decision 03 · Verification

Design for mistakes and recovery, not just successful entry.

The contact-verification journey includes OTP entry, validation feedback, resend behavior and a way to correct the mobile number.

Design rationale

Verification errors should explain what went wrong and keep users in the same task, rather than forcing them to restart their journey.

Decision 04 · Payment confidence

Explain the cost and what happens after payment.

Wedding details are collected before checkout; a payment summary gives users a final chance to review their choice. Separate success and failure states clarify the outcome.

Design rationale

Showing a breakdown before payment supports informed decisions. Explicit outcome states help users understand whether to wait for the expert or retry the transaction.

07 / Outcomes & measurement

What the design delivers — and what I'd measure next.

The documented output is a connected set of screens and interaction states. Conversion uplift, usability scores and payment performance have not been provided, so this case study does not claim measured improvements.

01 / DISCOVERY

Selection clarity

Measure progression from package or individual-service browsing to a completed selection.

02 / VERIFICATION

Task completion

Measure OTP completion, invalid-code frequency and abandonment during verification.

03 / TRANSACTION

Checkout confidence

Measure payment completion, failure recovery and whether users understand the next step.

08 / Reflection

What this project taught me about designing services.

01

A PRD is a starting point.

Requirements define what needs to happen; design makes the interactions and decisions understandable.

02

Edge states are part of the product.

Invalid OTPs, incomplete selections and failed payments deserve as much care as the successful journey.

03

Digital and human experiences must connect.

The interface should explain what the Relationship Manager will do after the user completes the transaction.

04

Good design is collaborative.

Product requirements, design reviews and engineering handoff all shape the final customer experience.
