01 / Product context

Wedding planning involves more than finding a vendor.

Couples navigate many service categories, coordinate with different vendors and make high-stakes decisions about money, dates and trust. Most of them don't need more options, they need someone to narrow things down. WedAssist does that with a dedicated Relationship Manager, but a service that mixes online buying with a human follow-up creates three problems for the interface.

01 / CHOICE

Multiple services

Couples need a way to understand available assistance and choose the categories relevant to their wedding.

02 / COORDINATION

Many moving parts

Vendor communication, recommendations and booking support need to connect to the choices made online.

03 / CONFIDENCE

High-stakes decisions

Before paying, users need to understand their selection, the amount due and what happens next.

Design challenge — How might we make assisted wedding planning feel as straightforward as booking a service online, without losing the reassurance of human support?

02 / My role & constraints

Turning product requirements into a complete experience.

The product team gave me the requirements and PRD direction. I turned them into screens, connected interactions and the states in between. I was the only designer on it: I reviewed the work with my design manager, worked through scope with the PM, and signed the designs off to engineering.

What I owned

UX/UI design for service and package selection, category drawers, contact verification, wedding details, checkout, payment feedback and handoff-related messaging.

How the team contributed

Product team: requirements and PRD direction.
Design manager: reviews and design direction.
Engineering: implementation, from my signed-off designs.

The PM owns the product metrics and they haven't been shared with me, so this case study doesn't claim measured results.

04 / Key design decisions

Four decisions that shaped the experience.

These are the areas where the product requirements became specific interface patterns, interaction rules and feedback states.

Decision 01 · Service selection

Make the offer understandable before asking users to commit.

I led with the assisted-service packs and kept a clear second path for people who want individual services.

Design rationale

Showing them as two distinct ways to buy help lets people choose before they get into details, instead of finding out halfway through.

Decision 02 · Focused customization

Keep service-category selection in context.

Category selection lives in a side drawer, so people stay anchored to the pack they chose and see their selections respond immediately.

Design rationale

The submit button stays disabled until the requirement is met, then enables, so the screen tells people when they're done instead of making them guess.

Decision 03 · Verification

Design for mistakes and recovery, not just successful entry.

Verification covers OTP entry, validation feedback, resend, and a way to correct a wrong mobile number.

Design rationale

An invalid code explains what went wrong and keeps people in the same task, rather than sending them back to the start. A person who gives up at the OTP step is one the Relationship Manager never gets to call.

Decision 04 · Payment confidence

Explain the cost and what happens after payment.

I collect the wedding date and city before checkout and show a review summary with the pack and amount, so the payment screen has no surprises. Success and failure are separate states.

Design rationale

Success explains what the expert does next. Failure gives a clear retry path. Either way, the user always knows whether to wait or try again.

Between the decisions

The small details that carry the experience.

Most of the work in a flow like this sits in small moments. These are the ones I cared about most, all visible in the screens above.

01 / THE RULE UP FRONT

State the requirement before the first tick

The category drawer lists eight services, each with an icon and a checkbox, and puts "Any 3 or more services of your choice" right under the title.

02 / THE BUTTON THAT TELLS YOU

Submit says when you're done

Submit stays grey until the selection is valid, then turns the brand colour, so people never wonder whether they've chosen enough.

03 / FIX, DON'T RESTART

A wrong number is one tap away

The OTP sheet shows the mobile number with an edit icon beside it, so a typo means correcting one field, not starting the journey again.

04 / PLAIN ERRORS

Say what's wrong, in plain words

An invalid code turns the four boxes red and says "Please enter a valid OTP". It doesn't show an error code or leave people guessing.

05 / A VISIBLE CLOCK

Show when you can ask again

A countdown sits beside "Didn't receive OTP?", so people know whether to wait or request a new code.

06 / A PERSON NEARBY

A help line in the header

"Need help? Call" stays in the header through verification and payment, a small reminder that a human is behind this service.

When it goes wrong, and when it goes right. A failed payment says "Your payment didn't go through. Please try again." with one clear action, Retry. A successful one says the Wedding Planning Expert will reach out soon, so the user knows the next step is a call, not another form.

07 / Outcomes & measurement

What the design delivered, and what I'd measure next.

The documented result is a connected set of screens and states, now part of a live product. The PM owns the conversion and payment numbers and they haven't been shared with me, so I don't claim figures I can't show.

Recognition from leadership. At a monthly performance review, our CEO appreciated WedAssist as a good initiative that was going well and bringing in clients. That is praise, not a measured result.

If I were measuring it, I would track:

01 / DISCOVERY

Selection clarity

Measure progression from package or individual-service browsing to a completed selection.

02 / VERIFICATION

Task completion

Measure OTP completion, invalid-code frequency and abandonment during verification.

03 / TRANSACTION

Checkout confidence

Measure payment completion, failure recovery and whether users understand the next step.

08 / Reflection

What this project taught me about designing services.

01

A PRD is a starting point.

Requirements define what needs to happen; design makes the interactions and decisions understandable.

02

Edge states are part of the product.

Invalid OTPs, incomplete selections and failed payments deserve as much care as the successful journey.

03

Digital and human experiences must connect.

The interface should explain what the Relationship Manager will do after the user completes the transaction.

04

Good design is collaborative.

Product requirements, design reviews and engineering handoff all shape the final customer experience.
