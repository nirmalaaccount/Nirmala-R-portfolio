01 / The starting point

I was looking for a trust problem. I found a timing question.

I started by reviewing Meesho's shopping experience, expecting to find missing information around trust. But the app already includes ratings, reviews, seller details and delivery information.

That changed the direction of my work. Rather than proposing more features, I became interested in when shoppers see the information that helps them choose.

What if a shopper's goal — not just the product category — influenced the way they browse?

Important distinction: This is an independent exploration. The design opportunities below are hypotheses, not verified Meesho usability failures.
02 / Early signals

Two shopping stories that made me reconsider the journey.

01 / Occasion

A shopper was more cautious when buying clothing for work or a wedding than when buying low-risk everyday items.

02 / Urgency

Another shopper chose a faster-delivery option elsewhere, even though the Meesho product was cheaper.

03 / Interpretation

Price matters, but relevance can also depend on delivery, confidence and the reason someone is shopping.

Small qualitative inputs can suggest a direction. They cannot prove how millions of shoppers behave.

These conversations were exploratory. I would need a broader study before treating the patterns as representative.

03 / The opportunity

Three moments. One connected journey.

Discover a starting point→Shortlist relevant products→Decide with confidence

I mapped the exploration around decisions instead of screens. This gave me a way to connect contextual discovery, product cards and product detail without treating them as unrelated redesigns.

04 / Selected explorations

Designing for the moment
of decision.

01 / PRIORITY ONE

Show delivery when it matters.

On product listings, I explored making delivery estimates easier to scan alongside price and ratings. This direction connects most directly to the urgency-related behavior from my early conversations.

Trade-off: More information can make cards harder to scan. I would test the smallest useful set of signals first.
Final design · Home / Discovery + PLPProduct cards, delivery visibility and intent-led discovery.
02 / NEXT

Make product details easier to evaluate.

I grouped the product detail experience around questions shoppers are likely to ask: Will it fit? When will it arrive? What do reviews say? Can I return it?

Trade-off: I would keep optional styling and inspiration modules secondary until research shows they help decisions.
Final design · Product Detail PageProduct information, delivery, sizing and customer proof.
03 / FUTURE EXPERIMENT

Start with an occasion, not a keyword.

For a shopper planning for Navratri, a contextual entry point could help surface relevant categories without requiring a precise search term.

Trade-off: Meesho already offers search, categories and promotions. This would need to outperform those existing journeys to justify its complexity.

05 / The product call

What I would build first — and what I wouldn't.

My first release would focus on product-card decision signals, especially delivery visibility. It's a narrower experiment, easier to evaluate than launching a completely new shopping mode.

I would defer community-led discovery and advanced styling experiences. They may be useful, but I don't yet have enough evidence to prioritize them over basic purchase decisions.

Delivery data also needs operational accuracy. A good interface cannot compensate for a promise the logistics system cannot support.

The alternatives I considered

Why product-card signals first?

The early conversations suggested a timing problem, but did not establish the best solution. I considered three ways to make delivery information useful without asking shoppers to relearn Meesho.

Chosen for first test

Delivery on listing cards

Make an estimated delivery date visible during comparison, alongside price and rating.

Why first: The shopper can use the signal before opening every product. It is a focused change that can be compared with the current listing.

Alternative to test later

Delivery-first filter

Let shoppers narrow results to products arriving by a deadline.

Why not first: It adds a step and depends on shoppers knowing to activate the filter. It may work well for urgent missions, but needs evidence.

Deferred

Intent-led shopping mode

Offer discovery entry points for occasions, budgets and time-sensitive needs.

Why later: It changes more of the discovery experience and is harder to attribute to a single behavioral improvement.

These are proposed alternatives and prioritization judgments—not solutions validated through an experiment.

07 / Validation & reflection

Still a hypothesis.
That's the point.

This concept hasn't been shipped or tested with a structured usability study, so I can't claim conversion or engagement improvements.

My next step would be to compare current and proposed product cards through tasks such as finding an outfit that arrives before a deadline. I would observe whether shoppers notice and understand the delivery information, and whether it helps them shortlist products.

The most important learning from this project wasn't a new feature. It was recognizing that a good audit can change the question you're trying to solve.

Better product design starts with a better question.
A testable next step

How I would know it works.

I would test the existing listing against the delivery-visible version with shoppers who have a specific arrival deadline. The goal is not to prove that the redesign looks better; it is to see whether it supports a more confident decision.

Primary task measure

Can the shopper correctly shortlist an item that meets a stated delivery deadline?

Decision efficiency

Time and product-detail visits needed to find a suitable option.

Comprehension check

Does the shopper understand the date as an estimate rather than a guarantee?

Guardrail

Does extra card information reduce scanability or lead shoppers to overlook price and rating?

Proposed validation sequence

  1. Give participants a realistic task: find an outfit under a budget that should arrive before a named event.
  2. Compare the current and proposed listing layouts using equivalent product information.
  3. Observe shortlisting behavior, misunderstandings and trade-offs—not just stated preferences.
  4. Iterate the hierarchy, then evaluate any live experiment only if delivery estimates are reliable.

Research plan only. No usability results, A/B-test results or business lift are claimed.
