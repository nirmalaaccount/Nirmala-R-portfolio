01 · Overview

A quick look at the project

THE CHALLENGE

Meesho already gives shoppers a lot of information.

While reviewing the product journey, I noticed that the information is there, but it is spread across different parts of the experience. I wanted to explore whether those signals could work together more naturally when someone is deciding what to buy.

THE CONCEPT

A more connected shopping experience.

Instead of redesigning the whole marketplace, I focused on one part of the journey: helping a shopper move from liking a product to feeling confident enough to buy it.

02 · Business Context

Why this is worth solving

I wanted the project to make sense from both sides of the screen. For a shopper, the goal is a confident decision. For the business, the same experience should create useful engagement without encouraging low-quality purchases.

USER GOALS

What is the shopper trying to do?

They want to find something relevant, understand whether it is right for them, see what other customers experienced, and sometimes get a little help deciding what to buy with it.

BUSINESS HYPOTHESIS

What does the business need?

The concept should be useful to the business too: better product engagement and conversion are possible outcomes, while return and cancellation rates remain important guardrails.

Business evidence: Meesho has publicly described how personalization and user behavior influence product discovery, feed relevance and conversion. Its personalization work uses early interactions such as product clicks, time spent on listings and scroll patterns to make the shopping feed more relevant to individual users. Direct product observations, public company statements, existing interface patterns and documented experiments.

What is not evidence?

Assumptions about what “all users” think, invented survey percentages, or guaranteed revenue impact from a concept.

06A · Qualitative Research

Real shopping experiences shaped the direction.

I used two real shopping experiences to understand what makes a purchase feel risky, what makes users switch marketplaces, and which signals matter when the decision becomes more important or time-sensitive.

STORY 01 · PURCHASE CONFIDENCE

Important purchases felt too risky.

One user said she avoids using Meesho for office wear and wedding wear because of concerns around product quality, delivery taking around a week, and the possibility of receiving the wrong product.

She is more comfortable using Meesho for kitchen products, casual items, or products needed for one-time use, where the purchase feels less risky.

Design insight: For higher-consideration purchases, users need confidence that the product, quality, delivery and purchase experience will meet their expectations.
STORY 02 · DELIVERY + VALUE

Urgency changed the decision.

Another user needed white jeans urgently. Meesho offered a lower price, but delivery would take around a week. Myntra was faster but more expensive. Ajio offered a combination of reasonable price, good quality and next-day delivery, so she chose Ajio.

Design insight: Price is only one part of the decision. When a purchase is urgent, users also consider delivery speed and confidence in the product.
Research note: This is early qualitative input from a small number of real conversations and one design-test session. It is directional evidence used to shape and refine the concept, not a statistically representative study or proof of product impact.
07 · Evidence

What the current Meesho experience already does well.

Before designing anything new, I wanted to understand the existing shopping journey. I used two current Meesho screens as the baseline: the product listing page and the product detail page.

Current PLP · DiscoveryCategories, offers, filters, product cards, price, ratings and marketplace signals are already part of the browsing experience.
Current PDP · Product decisionSize, seller, product details, similar products, ratings, customer photos, delivery and purchase actions are already available.
The audit changed my framing. The problem wasn't that Meesho had no useful information. It was that the shopping experience could do more to connect that information around the decisions a shopper is actually trying to make.
What this evidence tells me: the concept should build on the existing product experience rather than pretend that trust, reviews, delivery or seller information are missing.
PLP · DISCOVERY

Make decision signals visible earlier

While browsing a listing, users are scanning many products before deciding what is worth opening. I explored bringing value, trust and delivery signals into the browsing moment instead of making the shopper open every PDP to find them.

Design hypothesis: showing an eligible Get it by Tomorrow signal earlier may help shoppers judge relevance when delivery timing matters.

PDP · DECISION

Bring confidence closer to purchase

On the product page, delivery, seller, reviews, product evidence and returns already exist. I reorganized the experience so the most decision-relevant signals are easier to scan before the purchase action.

Design principle: improve the hierarchy of existing information before adding more.

11 · Product Strategy

A few principles kept the solution focused.

01

Confidence over overload

Prioritize information based on the user's decision, not the amount of information available.

02

Assist, don't interrupt

AI appears when the user has a real question or difficult-to-express intent.

03

Inspiration must stay shoppable

Styling content should lead naturally back to products and user-controlled actions.

Strategy statement

Turn product information + assistance + inspiration into a clearer path toward confident purchase.

The final screens.

DESIGN DECISION · PLP

Why redesign the PLP?

“The PLP is where the shopper decides which products are worth exploring. I wanted to make the most useful decision signals visible earlier — without adding unnecessary complexity.”

01 · DELIVERY

Show timing earlier

Eligible products surface Get it by Tomorrow, so delivery timing can be considered while browsing instead of only after opening a product.

02 · TRUST

Make confidence scannable

Ratings, trusted-seller cues, real content and return-related signals are treated as quick-to-scan context while comparing products.

03 · DISCOVERY

Help users shortlist

Budget, deals, community content and curated collections create clearer paths for exploration while keeping the core shopping interaction familiar.

Design hypothesis: surfacing these signals earlier may help users narrow choices with less effort. This is a hypothesis to validate, not a proven conversion outcome.

DESIGN DECISION · PDP

Why redesign the PDP?

“Once a shopper opens a product, the question changes from ‘Is this worth exploring?’ to ‘Do I feel confident enough to buy it?’ The PDP should support that decision without making the user hunt for evidence.”

01 · PRODUCT

Make the product easier to understand

Keep the core product, price, size and key attributes easy to scan before deeper information.

02 · CONFIDENCE

Bring proof closer to purchase

Seller context, ratings, real customer content, delivery and returns are organized around the purchase decision rather than treated as disconnected sections.

03 · CONTEXT

Add inspiration without taking over

Styling and similar-product content support exploration, while the primary purchase path remains clear and user-controlled.

Design principle: improve the hierarchy of existing information before adding more. The PDP changes are intended to make confidence easier to scan, not to claim that every user will purchase more.

15 · Design Decisions

Two surfaces, one decision journey.

The important design choice was not adding more features everywhere. I treated the PLP and PDP as two different decision moments and assigned each surface a clear job.

01 · PLP

Shortlist with context

The PLP prioritizes the signals a shopper can use without opening every product: delivery timing, value, trust, ratings and relevant discovery.

Decision: surface Get it by Tomorrow and trust/value cues directly in browsing.

02 · PDP

Decide with confidence

The PDP brings product evidence, seller context, delivery, reviews, returns and size information closer to the purchase decision.

Decision: reorganize existing confidence signals before introducing additional complexity.

03 · STYLING

Keep inspiration contextual

Styling content belongs after the core product decision is clear. It supports exploration rather than competing with the purchase path.

Decision: make inspiration shoppable but optional.

04 · AI

Keep AI task-based

AI is positioned around concrete shopping jobs such as finding products, styling an item or answering a product question rather than becoming a generic chatbot.

Decision: give AI a clear job inside the journey.

“I was not trying to make Meesho look like another marketplace. I was trying to make the existing shopping information easier to use at the moment a decision is being made.”

18 · Learnings

What I took away from the project

01

More information does not automatically mean more confidence

The useful question is whether the right signal appears when the user needs it.

02

Trust depends on the question the shopper is asking

Ratings, reviews, seller and delivery signals answer different questions. Their value depends on timing and hierarchy.

03

Cross-selling needs a reason

A complete-look concept works only when the complementary product has a clear relationship to the current product.

04

AI needs a useful job to do

“Ask AI anything” is vague. “Find a wedding outfit under ₹1,500” is a product problem worth solving.

I started this project thinking about trust. I ended up thinking more about decision-making. Meesho already has many of the ingredients; the opportunity is to connect them in a way that feels useful rather than simply adding more features.
