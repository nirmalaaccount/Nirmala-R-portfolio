01 · Overview

A simple question.

How might Meesho help shoppers move from “I have a shopping need” to “I found the right product and feel ready to buy”?

Discover

Give users useful ways to start shopping.

Explore

Turn an intent such as an occasion into relevant discovery.

Decide

Help users evaluate the product before purchase.

Scope: This is a self-initiated concept. I did not have Meesho internal analytics or large-scale customer access. The final designs are hypotheses, not proven business results.
02 · Business Context

Why this problem?

Meesho already offers a large assortment, categories, promotions, ratings, reviews, Gold/Premium signals and multiple ways to discover products. The shopping journey does not always begin with an exact product search.

A shopper might start with an occasion, a budget, a delivery deadline or simply inspiration. I wanted to explore how those different intents could become clearer entry points into the same familiar commerce journey.

The goal was not “add more features.” It was give existing shopping signals a clearer job at each decision point.
03 · Market Landscape

I reviewed Myntra, AJIO, Amazon, Flipkart and fast-delivery patterns to understand how commerce products handle discovery, delivery visibility, promotions, content and purchase confidence.

06 · Research Findings

Two observations shaped the direction.

STORY 01 · CONFIDENCE

Lower price does not remove uncertainty.

One shopper was comfortable using Meesho for lower-risk purchases but was more cautious about office and wedding wear because of concerns around quality, receiving the expected product and delivery.

Design insight: For higher-consideration purchases, price is only one part of confidence.
STORY 02 · URGENCY

Delivery can change the decision.

Another shopper needed white jeans urgently. Meesho was cheaper, but slower, so a faster marketplace became the better choice.

Design insight: When a purchase has a deadline, delivery can influence which product enters the shortlist.
07 · Evidence

Current strength

Current strength

The opportunity I chose was to connect these strengths around different shopping intents rather than treating every user as if they start with the same search behaviour.

15 · Design Decisions · Home / PLP

What I changed on the discovery layer — and why.

This is the main walkthrough of my first final design. I kept the familiar shopping model, but changed the hierarchy and entry points around different shopping intents.

01 · SHOPPING MODES

Bring the main shopping modes upfront

Meesho already has propositions such as Meesho, Mall, Prime and Minutes across the ecosystem. I explored bringing the key modes together at the top: Meesho · Minutes · Prime · Mall.

Why: Let users understand the different ways they can shop before going deeper into the journey.

02 · MINUTES

Make delivery speed a shopping choice

I did not invent Minutes. I made the fast-delivery proposition a prominent entry point because speed can be a shopping intent by itself.

Why: A user with an urgent need can choose a speed-led path upfront instead of discovering delivery options later.

03 · INTENT

Give users different ways to start

Personalized Picks, Budget Buys, Express Delivery, Deal of the Day and curated collections support different reasons to browse.

Why: Not every shopper arrives with a precise product keyword.

04 · PROMOTIONS

Make deals directly shoppable

Instead of using a promotion only as a banner, Flash Deals shows the products, discount, price, delivery and rating together.

Why: Reduce the gap between seeing an offer and understanding what can actually be bought.

05 · DELIVERY

Bring delivery into the product card

The final card surfaces cues such as Get it by 3 Oct closer to the product, rather than making users open every PDP to check timing.

Why: Delivery can affect shortlisting, especially when the shopper has a deadline.

06 · CUSTOMER PROOF

Bring customer content closer to discovery

I used ratings, customer-photo cues and Community Styling & Reviews as lightweight proof while users are still browsing.

Why: Help users judge whether a product is worth opening before they commit more time.

07 · PRODUCT CARD

Make the card decision-oriented

The familiar card remains: image, name, price and rating. Around that, I added context such as trending/bestseller, delivery and trust cues.

Why: Improve the information hierarchy without making the commerce pattern unfamiliar.

08 · COMMUNITY

Use content as discovery, not only review

Community Styling & Reviews brings visual content into the browsing journey.

Why: Customer content can help users discover products through inspiration as well as evaluate them after opening the PDP.

The core PLP decision: I didn't want to add more content just to make the home page richer. I wanted each module to support a different reason to start shopping — occasion, budget, speed, deals or inspiration.
16 · Design Decisions · Occasion

Turn an occasion into a shopping journey.

The occasion page is a supporting concept. I used Navratri as a concrete example of how an intent can become a structured discovery experience.

Navratri Utsav
Fashion, jewellery, beauty, decor, gifts and relevant curated products turn the occasion into a shopping destination.
WHY THIS DESIGN

The user knows the occasion, not necessarily the product.

Someone may think “I need to shop for Navratri” without knowing whether they want a kurti, jewellery, footwear, beauty products or decor.

Decision: Organize the experience around the occasion first, then help the user move into relevant categories and products.

Reusable: The same structure could support Diwali, Raksha Bandhan, wedding season, Pongal, back-to-college and other contextual shopping moments.

Important framing: I am not claiming that Meesho lacks festive merchandising. My exploration was to turn an occasion entry into a more complete discovery journey.
17 · Design Decisions · PDP

From product information to purchase decision.

The PDP is my second major design. Once the user opens a product, the job changes from discovery to evaluation. I reorganized the page around the questions a shopper needs to answer before buying.

Final PDP
Product → size → styling → delivery → seller → proof → purchase.
01 · PRODUCT + PRICE

Understand the product quickly

Product imagery, gallery, name, rating, price and discount are made easy to scan at the top.

02 · SIZE

Answer the size question early

Size selection sits close to the core product information because for fashion, size is part of deciding whether the product is usable for me.

03 · STYLING STUDIO

Show how the product can be worn

Styling Studio connects the product to different looks and answers the next question after “Do I like this?” — “How would I wear it?”

04 · STYLE THIS LOOK

Make inspiration shoppable

Complementary products are shown with individual prices and a total. Users choose what to add instead of automatically adding everything.

05 · TRANSPARENT PRICE

Make the total easy to understand

The complete look shows individual item prices and the combined total before the shopper commits.

06 · DELIVERY

Make the expected date easy to notice

Delivery is placed close to the buying decision because a deadline can determine whether the shopper continues.

07 · SELLER + PROOF

Keep marketplace confidence visible

Seller information, ratings, customer photos and reviews help the shopper judge both the product and the seller.

08 · SIMILAR PRODUCTS

Offer an alternative without restarting

Similar products give users another option if the current product is close but not quite right.

09 · PURCHASE ACTION

Keep Add to Cart and Buy Now available

After the user has evaluated the product, the purchase action should remain easy to reach.

The core PDP decision: I didn't add information just to make the page longer. I organized the experience around five questions: What is it? Will it work for me? When will I get it? Can I trust it? How can I style it?
18 · Validation

How I would test the design.

Because this is a self-initiated concept without internal analytics, I would not claim conversion lift. The next step is behavioural validation.

PLP TEST

Can users shortlist more easily?

Give users the same shopping task on the current and redesigned discovery experience. Observe time to shortlist and which signals they use.

OCCASION TEST

Can users discover without exact search?

Give users an occasion-based task and observe whether they can find relevant categories and products from the Navratri-style experience.

PDP TEST

Can users answer purchase questions faster?

Ask users to find size, delivery, seller, reviews, styling and product details. Compare task success with the current PDP.

Validation status: The final screens are design hypotheses. I am not claiming conversion lift, increased trust or business impact before behavioural testing.

20 · Learnings

What I learned.

01 · AUDIT FIRST

Don't assume the product needs more features.

I started by looking for missing trust and information. The audit showed that Meesho already had many of those capabilities.

02 · DESIGN CONTRIBUTION

A feature can exist while the experience can still be explored.

Minutes, Mall, Prime, ratings, reviews and categories already exist. My contribution was exploring how they could be surfaced and connected differently around shopping intent.

03 · DELIVERY

Delivery can influence discovery.

For a shopper with a deadline, delivery is not only a checkout detail. It can influence which product gets shortlisted.

04 · DIFFERENT JOBS

Every screen needs a clear job.

Home/PLP helps users discover and shortlist. Occasion gives discovery context. PDP helps users make the final product decision.

05 · HONEST VALIDATION

A finished screen is not proof.

The designs are hypotheses. Real impact would need broader usability testing, behavioural data and business guardrails.

06 · PRODUCT THINKING

Connect the journey, not just the screens.

The strongest part of the exploration is the connection between shopping intent, discovery, shortlisting and purchase decision.

“I didn't want to redesign Meesho feature by feature. I wanted to connect three moments: help users discover the right context, shortlist with useful signals, and make a confident product decision.”
